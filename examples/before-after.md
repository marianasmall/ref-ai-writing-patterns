# Before & After Examples

Concrete demonstrations of AI writing patterns and how to fix them.

## How to Use This Document

Each example shows:
- **Pattern** — Which of the 24 patterns is demonstrated
- **Context** — What type of content this appears in
- **Before** — Text with the pattern
- **After** — Revised text
- **Why it matters** — The impact of the change

---

## Example 1: Email Communication

### Pattern #21 (Filler Phrases) + Pattern #20 (Excessive Hedging)

**Context:** Professional email requesting a meeting

**Before:**
> Hi Sarah,
> 
> I hope this email finds you well. It's important to note that I wanted to reach out regarding the possibility of perhaps scheduling a brief meeting to discuss what might be some potential opportunities for collaboration between our teams. It should be emphasized that I think there could potentially be some interesting synergies worth exploring.
> 
> Would you possibly be available sometime next week for a quick conversation about this?

**After:**
> Hi Sarah,
> 
> I'd like to schedule a meeting to discuss collaboration opportunities between our teams. Are you available next week for a 30-minute conversation?

**Why it matters:** The original has 62 words to say what takes 26. Removing filler and hedging respects the recipient's time and sounds more confident.

**Patterns eliminated:** "I hope this email finds you well," "It's important to note that," "regarding the possibility of perhaps," "what might be some potential," "It should be emphasized that," "potentially," "possibly"

## Example 2: Marketing Copy

### Pattern #14 (Clichéd Metaphors) + Pattern #24 (Vapid Closing)

**Context:** Product announcement blog post

**Before:**
> Our new platform is a game-changer for the industry. It's a paradigm shift that moves the needle on productivity. We're not just thinking outside the box—we're reinventing the box itself.
> 
> This is just the tip of the iceberg. The possibilities are endless as we move forward into the future of work. The sky's the limit for what teams can achieve.

**After:**
> Our new platform reduces project completion time by 40% through automated workflow routing. Teams using it report fewer missed deadlines and clearer task ownership.
> 
> We're launching with three customers in Q1, scaling to general availability in Q2.

**Why it matters:** Concrete claims and data replace vague promises. The reader knows exactly what the product does and when they can use it.

**Patterns eliminated:** "game-changer," "paradigm shift," "moves the needle," "thinking outside the box," "tip of the iceberg," "possibilities are endless," "sky's the limit"

## Example 3: Technical Documentation

### Pattern #7 (Noun Clusters) + Pattern #6 (Passive Voice)

**Context:** Software documentation

**Before:**
> The customer relationship management system optimization process workflow can be initiated through the administrative dashboard interface configuration menu settings panel.

**After:**
> To optimize your CRM system:
> 1. Open the admin dashboard
> 2. Navigate to Configuration → Settings
> 3. Click "Optimize Workflow"

**Why it matters:** The noun cluster is nearly incomprehensible. The revision uses clear steps and active voice.

**Patterns eliminated:** "customer relationship management system optimization process workflow" (7 nouns stacked), passive construction "can be initiated"

## Example 4: Business Report

### Pattern #2 (Data Dumps) + Pattern #1 (Long Paragraphs)

**Context:** Quarterly performance report

**Before:**
> In Q1 2025, revenue was $5.2M. In Q2 2025, revenue increased to $6.1M. In Q3 2025, revenue reached $7.3M. In Q4 2025, revenue was $8.2M. The annual total for 2025 was $26.8M. Customer acquisition cost in Q1 was $340. In Q2 it was $380. In Q3 it was $410. In Q4 it was $395. Average customer lifetime value in Q1 was $1,200. In Q2 it was $1,350. In Q3 it was $1,420. In Q4 it was $1,490.

**After:**
> Revenue grew 58% in 2025, from $5.2M in Q1 to $8.2M in Q4, for a total of $26.8M.
> 
> Customer acquisition costs remained stable at $381 average across the year, while lifetime value increased 24% from $1,200 to $1,490.

**Why it matters:** The original lists 13 data points without interpretation. The revision extracts the story: growth rate, stability, and improving unit economics.

**Patterns eliminated:** Repetitive sentence structure, uninterpreted data points, dense paragraph

## Example 5: LinkedIn Post

### Pattern #22 (Generic Transitions) + Pattern #11 (Formulaic Triplets)

**Context:** Professional social media post

**Before:**
> In today's fast-paced business environment, success requires innovation, collaboration, and dedication. As we navigate the ever-changing landscape of modern work, it's clear that organizations must focus on three key areas: people, processes, and technology.

**After:**
> Last quarter, we cut project turnaround time by 30% by automating status updates. The change freed our team to focus on client strategy instead of paperwork.

**Why it matters:** The original could apply to any company in any industry. The revision shares a specific achievement with concrete results.

**Patterns eliminated:** "In today's fast-paced business environment," "as we navigate the ever-changing landscape," formulaic triplets (innovation/collaboration/dedication and people/processes/technology)

## Example 6: Article Introduction

### Pattern #4 (Surface-level -ing Verbs) + Pattern #5 (Weak Verb Substitutions)

**Context:** Thought leadership article

**Before:**
> The industry, symbolizing rapid change and reflecting modern consumer demands, acts as a catalyst for innovation. Companies, recognizing these shifts and responding to market pressures, serve as pioneers in transforming how products are delivered.

**After:**
> The industry is changing fast. Companies that recognize these shifts pioneer new ways to deliver products.

**Why it matters:** The -ing verbs and "serves as" constructions create distance from the action. The revision is direct and clear.

**Patterns eliminated:** "symbolizing," "reflecting," "recognizing," "responding," "acts as," "serve as"

## Example 7: Product Description

### Pattern #17 (False Scope Range) + Pattern #18 (Unnecessary Qualifiers)

**Context:** SaaS product landing page

**Before:**
> Our platform covers everything from basic task management to enterprise-level project portfolio optimization. It's somewhat flexible and quite powerful, providing fairly comprehensive features that are relatively easy to use for teams of all sizes, from small startups to large corporations.

**After:**
> Our platform handles:
> - Task assignment and tracking
> - Team workload balancing  
> - Project timeline visualization
> 
> Designed for teams of 5-500 people.

**Why it matters:** "Everything from X to Y" over-promises. The revised version states exactly what's included and who it's for.

**Patterns eliminated:** False scope claim, "somewhat," "quite," "fairly," "relatively"

## Example 8: Research Summary

### Pattern #19 (Listiness) + Pattern #15 (Uniform Sentence Structure)

**Context:** Academic-style report summary

**Before:**
> Key findings:
> - The study examined three variables
> - The research included 200 participants
> - The data showed significant correlation
> - The results indicate future research needed
> - The methodology employed mixed methods
> - The analysis revealed unexpected patterns

**After:**
> We studied three variables across 200 participants using mixed methods. The data showed significant correlation, though unexpected patterns suggest we need additional research to explain the mechanism.

**Why it matters:** Not everything needs bullets. The narrative version connects ideas and flows naturally.

**Patterns eliminated:** Unnecessary list format, repetitive sentence structure (The X verbed Y)

## Example 9: Meeting Follow-up

### Pattern #3 (Outdated Information) + Pattern #8 (Redundancies)

**Context:** Internal team communication

**Before:**
> Following up on our past history of discussions about the end result of the project timeline. As we collaborate together on future plans moving forward, it's important to finalize plans for the final deliverable.

**After:**
> Following up on our project timeline discussions. Next step: finalize the deliverable by Friday.

**Why it matters:** Every redundancy was removed. "Past history" → "history," "end result" → "result," "collaborate together" → "collaborate," "future plans" → "plans," "finalize plans" + "final deliverable" → one instance.

**Patterns eliminated:** "past history," "end result," "collaborate together," "future plans," "moving forward," "finalize" + "final" redundancy

## Example 10: Blog Post Closing

### Pattern #24 (Vapid Closing) + Pattern #13 (Robotic Transitions)

**Context:** Industry analysis blog post

**Before:**
> In conclusion, it's clear that the industry is evolving rapidly. Furthermore, companies must adapt to survive. Moreover, innovation will continue to be important. In addition, customer needs will drive change. The future looks bright for organizations that embrace these shifts. Only time will tell how things develop. The possibilities are endless.

**After:**
> Three companies have already adopted this approach: TechCorp reduced costs 35%, RetailCo expanded to five new markets, and ServiceCo doubled customer retention. Early results suggest this model works—but only when paired with operational discipline.

**Why it matters:** The original says nothing. The revision provides concrete evidence and ends with a specific, actionable insight.

**Patterns eliminated:** "In conclusion," "Furthermore," "Moreover," "In addition," "The future looks bright," "Only time will tell," "The possibilities are endless"

## Pattern Clusters in Real Content

Some patterns commonly appear together:

### Cluster 1: Hedging + Filler
Often seen in tentative professional communication:
- Pattern #18 (Qualifiers) + Pattern #20 (Hedging) + Pattern #21 (Filler phrases)

### Cluster 2: Hype + Clichés
Common in marketing and announcements:
- Pattern #14 (Clichéd metaphors) + Pattern #22 (Generic transitions) + Pattern #24 (Vapid closings)

### Cluster 3: Weak Language
Appears in passive, indirect writing:
- Pattern #4 (-ing verbs) + Pattern #5 (Weak verb substitutions) + Pattern #6 (Passive voice)

Recognizing these clusters helps you catch multiple patterns in one editing pass.

1. **Find patterns you hit most** — Review your recent writing. Which of these examples sound familiar?

2. **Study the transformations** — What specifically changed? Why did it improve?

3. **Create your own library** — Save before/after examples from your actual work

4. **Practice deliberately** — Take one pattern, write 10 before/after examples from your domain

5. **Internalize over time** — Eventually you'll write the "after" version naturally

## Context Matters

These are general examples. In specific contexts, some patterns are acceptable:

- **Technical documentation** may appropriately use passive voice and noun clusters
- **Academic writing** follows different conventions
- **Creative writing** might intentionally use patterns for effect
- **Transcripts** should preserve the speaker's natural voice

Use judgment. The goal is authentic communication, not mechanical pattern elimination.
